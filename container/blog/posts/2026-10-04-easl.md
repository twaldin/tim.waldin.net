The diagram was in one window. The page was in another. The note was in a third. The agent was in a fourth, and I was typing a description of the other three.

I gave up on the description.

Most of my time with coding agents now goes into the part after the code: working out what the agent did, how the pieces fit, and whether it's what I meant. The agent will explain if I ask. I get a few paragraphs and a pile of `path:line` references, sometimes an ASCII diagram. Then it scrolls away, and an hour later I ask again.

On Friday Andrej Karpathy [posted](https://x.com/karpathy/status/2105819303471976479) about this: "We'll be spending a lot more time trying to understand the outputs of language models." His tips climb from cleaner writing to diagrams to web pages ("Ask for output 'in HTML'") to explainer videos, and his summary is that more of our work "will rise up the abstractions into oversight and understanding."

I'd already been asking my agents for diagrams and HTML pages, and they're much easier to read than prose. My problem was where they end up. The diagram lands in the scrollback, and the page in a browser tab the agent can't see. When I want to ask about one box in it, I'm back to describing it.

## the transcript is a log

A chat transcript, in a GUI app or in a terminal, only grows at the bottom. It records what happened, in order, and everything I care about drifts up out of view as the session goes on.

What I've understood and what I mean is state: the diagram I'll want again, the button I'm talking about, the note that says what that button should look like. I want that state on a surface that stays put, that the agent can read and add to, and that I can point at. Karpathy calls these artifacts "discardable", and most of them are. A few I want to keep, because I'll point at them again.

## easl

easl is my attempt at that surface. It's a native Mac app with one infinite board per repository. Your agents run in real terminals on the board: your own `claude`, `codex` or any CLI, logged in as you. What they make lands beside their terminal: code tiles at exact lines with your language server, call graphs it computes, a browser, notes, HTML pages they write, and shapes and arrows.

Then you point. Hold ⌃⌥⇧⌘ and click a box in a diagram, an element on a page or an item in a note. Each click becomes a chip in a tray at the bottom of the window, and the next prompt you submit in that terminal carries them with their context. A shape brings what it encloses and what it connects to. A note item brings the headings above it. A page element brings its page. So the prompt can be "make this button match this box, like the note says".

It's free and MIT: [github.com/twaldin/easl](https://github.com/twaldin/easl), and [easl.sh](https://easl.sh).

## what I use it for

Most of my day in easl is a browser tile showing my ticket board, with my active tickets, their status and their PRs, beside the agents working on them. When I want an agent on a ticket, a PR or a change, I ⌃⌥⇧⌘-click it on the board, and it goes with my next prompt.

## how it works

Each terminal is libghostty, with your Ghostty config if you have one, running in a [zmx](https://github.com/neurosnap/zmx) session, so quitting or crashing easl doesn't stop the agent. Inside a tile, `claude` and `codex` are small wrappers that easl puts first on the PATH. They run your real binary with easl's hooks and skill for that session only, and nothing goes in your global agent, shell or Ghostty config. The hooks tell easl when an agent is working, blocked on you or done, and deliver your clicks with the next prompt. Agents build on the board through the `easl` CLI, over a local socket with a JSON schema. The [docs](https://easl.sh/docs/) have the rest.

## limits, and what I don't know yet

- macOS 14 or later on Apple silicon. No Intel, Linux or Windows build.
- Code tiles are read-only. You edit in your editor, or the agent writes the file.
- Call graphs need a language server with call hierarchy. I've checked sourcekit-lsp.
- The browser is WebKit, so Playwright and other CDP tools can't attach.
- Gemini CLI 0.60 and later runs as a plain terminal, without easl's hooks. ⌃⌥⇧⌘V pastes your clicks into it.
- No account and no API key. Your agents use the login they already have. easl has no AI of its own.
- I don't know yet how big a board can get before it's as hard to search as the scrollback.

## install

```sh
curl -fsSL https://easl.sh/install | sh
brew install neurosnap/tap/zmx oven-sh/bun/bun
```

The script is short and readable at [easl.sh/install.txt](https://easl.sh/install.txt). It downloads the release zip from GitHub, checks it against a pinned SHA-256 and moves `easl.app` to `/Applications` (or `~/Applications` if that isn't writable), with no sudo. Quit easl before you run it. curl doesn't set macOS's quarantine flag, so there's no Open Anyway step. Terminals need zmx. The `easl` CLI and every agent integration need bun.

Or download `easl-0.1.0.zip` from [releases](https://github.com/twaldin/easl/releases/tag/v0.1.0) and move `easl.app` to `/Applications`. The zip is ad-hoc signed. Clear the quarantine flag with `xattr -dr com.apple.quarantine /Applications/easl.app`, or open it once and choose Open Anyway in System Settings › Privacy & Security.

Then press ⌘T and run `claude` or `codex`. Codex asks whether to trust the folder. Answer in the terminal. Bugs and ideas go in [GitHub issues](https://github.com/twaldin/easl/issues).
